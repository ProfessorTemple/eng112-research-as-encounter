# My Living Field Notebook: pedagogical validation

Status: proposed instructor-led formative pilot, not conducted. No finding of improved learning, effectiveness, accessibility conformance, or readiness for public release is established by this protocol.

Companion documents: [student journey walkthrough](student-journey-walkthrough.md) (usability, not learning), [pedagogical restraint protocol](pedagogical-restraint-protocol.md) (quality of notebook responses), [release gates](living-notebook-release-gates.md) (technical and instructor approval).

## Purpose

Does the notebook help students notice, inquire, interpret, engage sources, revise their understanding, and eventually exercise those practices independently? The unit of concern is the student's reasoning, not button clicks, time on site, number of entries, vocabulary sophistication, or the amount of guidance accepted. A student's unchanged or qualified interpretation may be a strong outcome.

## Boundaries and participation

- First use the synthetic cases below with the instructor or another reviewer. Do not place experimental requirements into the current graded final essay or compel students to use the experimental notebook. Preserve all ordinary instruction and access to the stable alternative.
- A small, voluntary formative classroom pilot may follow instructor approval and the accessibility/reliability checks in the release gates. Students may decline or stop without penalty or explanation. Offer equivalent non-digital materials and accessibility accommodations. Do not recruit students to disclose personal memories, disability, identity or sensitive experiences.
- The notebook is not a research participant registry. Keep writing in the student's own browser or on their own paper; no analytics additions, automatic text collection, or submission. Do not require account identifiers, screenshots or JSON backups for evaluation.
- Ask students for optional feedback on the tool, not copies of their personal writing. The ordinary usability walkthrough records only non-identifying barriers. For this learning-focused pilot, use synthetic case responses and student self-review; record only aggregate, non-identifying observations. If individual artifacts, linked before/after records, recordings, systematic participant data, publication or generalizable research are desired, obtain the appropriate institutional determination/approval and consent first. Do not assume anonymous or educational activity is exempt. Instructor assessment of ordinary course assignments remains separate.
- No claims of mastery, grading, ranking, learner type, diagnosis or inferred cognitive growth. A software-generated question is not evidence of a student's understanding. Missing evidence is recorded as not observed, not failure.

## Three-part inquiry sequence (suggested 20–30 minutes; adjustable)

1. **Before: unaided encounter.** Give one synthetic case, a brief excerpt supplied with it, and the neutral prompt below. Students write or think in their preferred medium, without notebook assistance. Keep their response private.
2. **During: supported inquiry.** Use the experimental notebook with that case, or the existing paper-based course guidance. Students choose whether to use its help, record a source, or revisit a claim. Do not coach them toward particular concepts or a predetermined interpretation. Ask them to identify a specific detail or evidence that influenced their reasoning, if any.
3. **After: independent transfer.** Give the other case, with the same neutral prompt and no notebook assistance. Students use their own questions and practices. If feasible, repeat with a new comparable case in a later class to examine retention. No additional graded assignment or compulsory reflection.

For a small formative comparison, vary the order of the cases (A then B or B then A) and keep the prompt, approximate working time and source access comparable. Case order alone does not establish a causal effect. The same source repeated in the first two stages can produce practice effects. If a formal study is subsequently approved, compare a notebook-supported group with equivalent existing course guidance, consider assignment/order effects and accessibility, specify outcomes in advance, and seek independent review. Do not withhold essential course support.

**Neutral prompt for both cases:** What did you notice? What might it mean? How does the supplied account help you interpret it, complicate it, or leave it unresolved? Why might the interpretation matter? You can maintain your original interpretation if you can explain why.

## Two comparable fictional practice cases

These are original, synthetic practice materials, not published scholarship or real observations. They must not be cited as factual sources in course essays. The contextual notes are deliberately limited and do not establish what actually caused the events.

### Case A: creek after rain

**Encounter:** Yesterday afternoon, I stood beside a small creek after a rainstorm. At the bend, leaves gathered against a branch. A few passed under it; others remained in the shallows. The day before, I had walked by without noticing any leaves there. I found myself thinking the creek was holding onto them.

**Practice source note (fictional, paragraph 1):** In this imagined account, an observer describes how changes in the shape of a stream channel, the presence of branches and the direction of water movement might affect where floating objects accumulate. The observer cautions that a single visit cannot reveal which factor is most influential.

### Case B: birds at a courtyard

**Encounter:** One morning, I watched three birds near a courtyard tree. When a door opened, two moved to a higher branch and one stayed on the ground. A few minutes later the two came down again. I had expected every bird to react in the same way. I wondered whether the birds were warning one another.

**Practice source note (fictional, paragraph 1):** In this imagined account, an observer describes how an animal's responses might vary with its position, previous encounters, nearby activity and the signals it can perceive. The observer cautions that a single event does not establish whether one animal intentionally communicated with another.

Both cases allow sensory attention, alternative interpretations, metaphor or intention, evidence limitations and wider significance. They are not calibrated or empirically equivalent; revise them after instructor review rather than treating their difficulty as established.

## Qualitative review anchors

Use these as discussion and self-review criteria, not a grade or student profile. Mark a practice as observed, emerging, not observed, or not assessable only in an approved aggregate review. One sentence can demonstrate several practices; eloquence is not required. A brief or oral response may demonstrate the same reasoning as extended prose.

| Dimension | Emerging practice | Clear evidence of practice | Do not mistake for evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attentiveness | Names a detail | Distinguishes concrete details, conditions or overlooked perspectives from inferences | Sensory adjectives alone |
| Curiosity | Poses a question | Identifies a specific uncertainty or alternative that can be investigated | Number of questions |
| Conceptual fluency | Uses a term or distinction | Explains what the distinction makes visible and where it may not apply | Jargon or vocabulary count |
| Interpretive depth | Offers a possible meaning | Connects interpretation to a particular detail/source, considers alternatives or limits, and explains significance | Certainty, elaborate wording or agreement with the tool |
| Intellectual transformation | Describes a difference or continuity | Explains why an encounter, source or reasoning changed, qualified, or sustained an understanding | Changed wording alone or forced conversion |
| Independence | Recognizes a useful practice | Initiates a relevant inquiry practice on the new case without guidance and explains why it fits when asked | Number of prompts rejected, no help-seeking, or declaring mastery |

Review the quality of the notebook's invitations separately using the restraint protocol. Check for unsupported interpretations, fabricated source claims, imposed beliefs, excessive interventions or misleading assessment language. A student may reasonably interpret nonhuman agency, reciprocity or communication in more than one way; assess the relationship among their meaning, evidence and qualification, not adherence to a worldview.

## Optional student-facing feedback (do not require written submissions)

- What, if anything, helped you see something differently?
- Did a question help you think, or did it interrupt you?
- Did you feel free to disagree, decline help or keep your own interpretation?
- What question or practice would you take into another inquiry without the notebook?
- What felt confusing, inaccessible or unnecessary?

A student can answer none, some or all. Avoid prompts that presuppose the notebook was helpful.

## Aggregate decision record

Date/build; approved activity scope; methods used (technical, usability, formative learning); broad non-identifying observations; evidence supporting or challenging each design intention; accessibility barriers; proposed changes; unresolved questions; reviewer decision. No names, verbatim student prose, student-level scores, or small-cell demographic breakdowns.

Decision options: retain, simplify, revise, remove, or defer a feature; continue limited pilot, pause pilot, or recommend stable release subject to all gates. Block promotion for work loss, inaccessible core tasks, misleading privacy behavior, forced interpretations or automatic authorship intrusion. Mixed pedagogical observations warrant refinement rather than claims of success. Do not report learning gains from a handful of self-selected observations.
