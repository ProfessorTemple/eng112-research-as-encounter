Frequently asked questions · Saving, JSTOR, and your writing
Where do I add a JSTOR PDF?

On Start Here, open Add a JSTOR PDF from earlier work. Choose the article PDF, enter its title, and select Save PDF on this device. Once you enter the notebook, the PDF appears in Research → Reading Journal, where you can add its citation, verified passages, and your own interpretation. If your PDF is only inside an earlier browser shelf, download a copy from that earlier notebook first. A .json notebook backup does not contain PDFs.

Why would I use JSTOR?

JSTOR is a source of scholarly articles that can introduce perspectives you had not considered. Read the article itself and ask what it supports, challenges, or complicates about your original experience. Finding a source is the beginning of research, not proof of a claim.

Does uploading a PDF submit it to my instructor?

No. The optional PDF shelf stores a copy in this browser on this device only. It is not uploaded to PCC, GitHub, Moodle, Canvas, or your instructor. For instructor feedback, use Share journey to choose and review what you want to send separately.

What's the difference between Save backup and Save PDF?

Save backup downloads your notebook's research and writing data as a JSON file. It does not contain saved article PDFs. Save PDF on this device adds a downloaded article to the browser's optional shelf. Keep your original PDFs in a folder or cloud drive and make regular notebook backups too.

Will my work be there on another computer?

Not automatically. Notebook writing and optional PDF copies are stored locally in the browser. Export the full notebook backup, bring it to the other device, and use Import an existing Research as Encounter backup. Importing replaces the notebook project currently open in that browser, so back it up first. Transfer your article PDFs separately.

Must I complete every section in order?

No. Discover, Research, and Write are flexible spaces. Follow the path that helps you today, return to an earlier question whenever you need to, and use the Living Thesis Studio to reflect on how your thinking changes.

How do I know what to do after writing in a box?

Look for One possible next move at the end of the current page. The notebook will explain why that move might help and offer a way back. You can continue writing, choose another section, or return to an earlier question. Nothing is submitted automatically.

What are critical lenses, and what if the suggestions miss my meaning?

Critical lenses are different ways to ask questions about an experience. The Concept Explorer can suggest a few at a time, with a short explanation of each. You can select, reject, browse the complete interdisciplinary library, or write your own lens. A suggestion does not tell you what your experience means.

Does this notebook learn from my private writing?

This development version uses a local matching library, not an online AI model. Your selections and rejections can change later suggestions in this browser. They are not used to train a shared model, and this test page does not upload your freewriting or essays. Optional counts in GoatCounter are separate from your writing.

How do I find a reserve book, database article, or online essay?

For a reserve book, open the PCC book-search page, find its catalog record, and ask at the service desk. ENG 112 reserve copies must remain inside the library. For a database article, open the PCC database directory, select the named database, sign in if asked, and search the exact article title. For an open-web essay, use its original publication link. Confirm that you can access and read a source before citing it.

Can I compare thesis versions without losing a draft?

Yes. Save a stage of your thinking in the Living Thesis Studio. Thesis Comparison displays two stages side by side without overwriting either one. Loading an earlier version for editing is a separate action that asks for confirmation.

What if JSTOR does not let me download the PDF?

Keep the article's stable link and citation information, and add the source directly in the Reading Journal. Access may depend on the article and your PCC Library sign-in. You can still record notes based on an article you can legitimately read online.

Is my feedback anonymous? Can I skip it?

Feedback is optional and does not affect your grade. After exporting a document, the notebook may invite you once to rate it. You can choose Not now, and the invitation will not appear automatically again in this browser. If analytics are allowed, GoatCounter receives only predefined counts of invitations, ratings, and dismissals, never your writing. Optional written comments are not submitted by this notebook: you may copy them and decide whether to share them separately. We do not use the ratings to identify students, though aggregate counts cannot guarantee complete anonymity at the network level.

2. Shape your search

Start with one chosen keyword, then try combining two or three distinct concepts. AND usually narrows a search; OR usually broadens it. Put synonyms in the alternative fields. Your searches remain editable, and different PCC databases may interpret operators differently.

Open the ENG 112 PCC Library guide ↗ (internet and PCC login may be required).

Search operators: AND, OR, NOT, quotes, parentheses

AND narrows by requiring both concepts. OR broadens by allowing alternatives. NOT is available only through the separate exclusion field, since it can hide important results. Quotes search an exact phrase where the database supports it, and parentheses keep alternatives grouped. Database behavior varies.

Example: ("grandmothers" OR "intergenerational knowledge") AND gardening

Too many or too few results?

Too many: add one specific concept using AND, or use a phrase in quotation marks. Too few: remove one concept, add synonyms with OR, or try broader terms. Check article abstracts for better vocabulary. The program does not query PCC databases or verify subject headings.

Living Three-Story Thesis

Ready to write? Jump directly to a writing box. You can return to the guidance anytime.

1. Observation2. Interpretation3. SignificanceMy working thesis
Read about the Three-Story Thesis (Trisha Tucker)

A thesis can be several connected sentences rather than a single declaration. Think of it as a house: each floor needs the support of the floor below.

Third floor · Significance

Why does your interpretation matter? Connect it to a wider understanding without making a claim larger than your evidence supports.

Second floor · Interpretation

Offer a meaningful, supportable reading of what you noticed. Another reader might reasonably interpret it differently. Ask why, how, and to what effect.

First floor · Observation

Begin with a specific fact, detail, experience, or pattern that a reader can recognize or verify. This is the foundation, not yet the argument.

An ENG 112 example: listening to a dog

Observation: My dog pauses at the edge of the trail and looks back at me before continuing.

Interpretation: This repeated exchange invites me to reconsider our walk as a negotiation rather than an activity I alone direct.

Significance: Attending to such exchanges may help us rethink human authority and recognize the participation of other beings in shared places.

Illustrative example, not a research finding. Your own observations and sources must support your interpretation. The three floors work together; they need not become three separate paragraphs.

Reading guide paraphrased from the course handout The Three-Story Thesis, which credits Professor Trisha Tucker et al., Building a ‘Three-Story’ Thesis Statement. ENG 112 example added for this notebook.

My developing three-story movement

These are layers of thinking, not three mandatory essay sections. Each story must grow from the one before it. A research question is not yet a thesis; an interpretation makes a meaningful claim that can be investigated.

Give readers something particular to stand on: a scene, a pattern, a place, a detail, or a documented finding.

This is the intellectual turn. Offer an interpretation someone could reasonably question, and one your sources can help you investigate.

Open the view to a human, cultural, ecological, ethical, or philosophical implication. Avoid a grand claim unsupported by your observation and evidence.

Keep the questions alive. A living thesis can acknowledge what research cannot yet establish. Ground each interpretation in actual evidence; the third story is an earned implication, not automatically a call to action.

How can I tell if this is really a three-story thesis?

First story: Is there a particular observation or relationship, rather than just a broad topic?

Second story: Does my writing offer an interpretation that a thoughtful reader might question, or am I merely describing the first story again?

Third story: Does the larger significance grow from the interpretation, or have I made a sweeping claim that my evidence cannot support?

As a whole: Can I read the thesis as a coherent thought? Does it invite inquiry rather than announce certainty I have not earned?

Example: a three-story thesis growing from a creek encounter

First story: I had walked past the neighborhood creek for years without noticing the animals and debris gathering along its banks.

Second story: Hearing my father's memories of that same creek complicated my idea of it as neglected scenery, revealing it as a place shaped by labor, childhood, and environmental change.

Third story: This shift matters because the places we overlook may already be part of our communities' identities, and recognizing those relationships can change what we consider worthy of care.

This is a model of movement, not wording to copy. Its interpretation and significance must be supported by the particular writer's experience and evidence.
